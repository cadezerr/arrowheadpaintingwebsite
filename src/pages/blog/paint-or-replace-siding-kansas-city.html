Should you paint or replace your siding?

By Cade Zerr · October 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick Answer

If the siding is solid, paint it. Peeling, fading, and chalking are paint problems, not siding problems. Replace individual boards that are soft, swollen, cracked, or delaminating, and paint the whole house so the repairs blend in. Full replacement only makes sense when damage is spread across most of the house or the siding is failing as a product.

A lot of homeowners call us assuming they need new siding, when what they really need is paint and a dozen new boards. It goes the other way too. Sometimes a fresh coat would just be covering up a problem.

Here's how we tell the difference when we walk a house.

Start with one question: is the board solid?

Everything comes down to this. Paint can fix how siding looks and protect it from water. It can't fix wood that's already soft.

  • Solid board, ugly paint: paint it. Peeling, fading, chalky residue, and cracked caulk are all normal signs that the paint is worn out. Here's how to read them.
  • Soft, swollen, or crumbling board: replace that board, then paint.
  • Most of the house is damaged: now you're talking about full replacement.

Signs a board needs replacing

  • A screwdriver sinks in, or the wood feels spongy
  • Swelling or bubbling at the bottom edge, common on older engineered siding
  • Layers peeling apart, cracks running along the grain, or chunks missing
  • Dark staining around nail heads or seams that keeps coming back
  • Mushrooms or green growth (yes, we've seen it)

On Kansas City homes, the bad boards are usually in the same places: the bottom row near the ground, under windows, beside doors, and anywhere a gutter dumps water against the wall.

Why patch-and-paint usually wins

On most houses we look at, the damage is limited to a small share of the boards. Cutting those out, matching the profile, sealing every edge, and painting the whole house gives you a home that looks and performs like new without the cost and mess of tearing off every wall.

We handle that carpentry in-house, from siding and trim to fascia, soffits, and door and window trim, so there's no second contractor to schedule. If you're choosing materials for the new boards, our siding comparison walks through James Hardie, LP SmartSide, and cedar.

When full replacement makes sense

  • Rot or swelling shows up on most walls, not just the usual trouble spots
  • The siding is a product that's known to fail and is breaking down everywhere
  • You're remodeling or adding on and want a different look anyway
  • The boards no longer hold paint even after proper prep

A quick test you can do: walk the house with a flat screwdriver and press it into the bottom edge of the siding every few feet. If you find fewer than a handful of soft spots, you almost certainly need paint and repairs, not new siding.
